Abstract—Kidney diseases present a significant global health
challenge, traditionally requiring time-intensive manual interpre-
tation of radiological imaging. Deep learning (DL) has emerged as
a transformative approach for the automated diagnosis, segmen-
tation, and prognostic evaluation of renal pathologies. However,
existing literature often isolates single tasks, lacking a cohesive
review of multi-class classification and surgical planning integra-
tion. This survey systematically reviews 63 recent studies focusing
on DL applications in kidney disease diagnosis, primarily utilizing
CT imaging. We analyze core architectural evolutions, spanning
convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to hybrid Transformer
models, and evaluate their performance on benchmark datasets.
Furthermore, we critically examine the integration of Explainable
AI (XAI) and the transition from isolated bounding-box detection
to holistic, 3D multimodal clinical frameworks. We identify
current research gaps, proposing a unified framework to bridge
the gap between computational accuracy and clinical utility.
Index Terms—Deep Learning, Kidney Disease, Computed To-
mography (CT), Explainable AI (XAI), Vision Transformers,
Surgical Planning.
I. INTRODUCTION
Kidney diseases, encompassing both neoplastic formations
(such as renal cell carcinoma) and non-neoplastic conditions
(such as nephrolithiasis and complex cysts), represent a mount-
ing global health challenge. Precise and timely diagnostic
workflows are critical for patient survival and effective surgi-
cal intervention. Traditionally, the identification, staging, and
volumetric analysis of these pathologies have relied almost
exclusively on the manual inspection of Computed Tomogra-
phy (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans by
expert radiologists. This conventional paradigm is inherently
subjective, highly time-consuming, and susceptible to inter-
observer variability, particularly when differentiating subtle
malignant subtypes or delineating complex, multi-focal tumor
boundaries for partial nephrectomies.
In recent years, the integration of deep learning (DL)
into medical image analysis has fundamentally disrupted this
diagnostic bottleneck [1]. DL architectures, specifically those
engineered for advanced computer vision tasks, excel at ex-
tracting high-dimensional, non-linear semantic features from
complex anatomical structures. While early computational
applications in nephrology focused predominantly on binary
classification (e.g., normal versus abnormal tissue), the current
clinical frontier demands highly granular multi-class classi-
fication, exact 3D volumetric segmentation for preoperative
planning, and robust algorithmic interpretability. A vast and
accelerating body of recent literature has empirically validated
the efficacy of these automated approaches across diverse
clinical datasets, varying noise profiles, and different stages
of disease progression [2]–[9].
Despite accelerating algorithmic advancements, a critical
translational gap remains between high-performing computa-
tional models and their actual deployment in clinical environ-
ments. Many contemporary models function as opaque ”black
boxes” [10], lacking the diagnostic explainability required to
build trust among medical practitioners. Furthermore, diagnos-
tic networks are frequently trained on narrow, homogeneous
datasets, which severely limits their ability to generalize across
diverse clinical hardware and disparate patient demographics.
This survey comprehensively synthesizes the methodolo-
gies, computational complexities, and clinical integrations of
63 recent DL frameworks. By meticulously analyzing the
trajectory from traditional convolutional networks to advanced
hybrid Transformer models, this paper aims to establish a
clear taxonomy of current progress. Ultimately, we identify
existing structural gaps and chart the necessary trajectory
toward clinically viable, explainable AI (XAI) systems that
integrate seamlessly into real-world renal care pipelines.

B. Data Extraction and Matrix Synthesis
Following the removal of duplicates, non-peer-reviewed
preprints lacking methodological rigor, and studies with insuf-
ficient computational detail, 63 primary papers were selected
for deep extraction. For each study, data was systematically
mapped into a comprehensive literature matrix. The extracted
parameters include the imaging modality, primary pathology
focus, dataset characteristics (e.g., benchmark challenge data
versus proprietary clinical data), core model architecture, com-
putational complexity, integration of Explainable AI (XAI),
and identified deployment limitations.
III. KEYDATASETS ANDBENCHMARKS
The evolution of deep learning in renal imaging is in-
trinsically linked to the availability of large-scale, anno-
tated datasets. Historically, algorithmic development was con-
strained by small, proprietary clinical datasets, which limited
reproducibility and generalizability. The introduction of open-
source challenges has since standardized benchmarking for
segmentation and classification tasks.
A. The KiTS Challenge Series
The Kidney Tumor Segmentation (KiTS) challenges rep-
resent the most significant catalyst for standardizing renal
imaging benchmarks.
•KiTS19:Introduced by Heller et al. [?], this dataset pro-
vided 210 high-quality, multiphase CT scans with voxel-
level annotations for both the kidney and renal tumors. It
established the foundational baseline for 3D segmentation
architectures, predominantly evaluating variations of the
3D U-Net.
•KiTS21:An expansion of its predecessor, KiTS21 [11]
introduced a more complex hierarchy of annotations,
incorporating multiple annotators to account for inter-
observer variability. The dataset shifted the focus from
simple bounding-box detection to highly granular, multi-
class semantic segmentation, making it the primary
benchmark for the majority of the state-of-the-art seg-
mentation frameworks reviewed in this survey.
B. Kaggle CT Kidney Dataset
While KiTS focuses heavily on segmentation, the Kaggle
CT Kidney Dataset [?] has become a standard benchmark for
multi-class classification. It typically comprises thousands of
2D axial slices categorized into four primary classes: Normal,
Cyst, Tumor, and Stone. Models evaluated on this dataset,
such as those utilizing attention-enhanced networks, frequently
report accuracy metrics exceeding 95%, demonstrating robust
capability in distinguishing distinct pathologies from 2D slices.
C. Proprietary and Multimodal Data
Despite the prevalence of public datasets, several studies
rely on proprietary, multi-center clinical data to validate mod-
els in real-world scenarios. For instance, frameworks designed
for surgical planning or multimodal integration (combining CT
with MRI) often utilize private datasets to capture a broader
spectrum of patient demographics and scanning hardware
variations [12], [13]. However, the reliance on private data
introduces significant challenges in algorithmic transparency
and peer-review reproducibility.

IV. DEEPLEARNINGARCHITECTURES: A CATEGORICAL
REVIEW
The computational approach to renal imaging has undergone
a significant paradigm shift over the last five years. Early
diagnostic automation relied heavily on classical machine
learning, utilizing hand-crafted feature extraction methods
such as Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrices (GLCM) paired
with Support Vector Machines [14]. While interpretable, these
methods lacked the semantic depth required to navigate the
high variability of renal pathologies across diverse patient
demographics.
The advent of deep learning largely replaced these classical
pipelines. Based on our systematic review of 63 studies,
contemporary architectures can be distinctly categorized by
their foundational feature-extraction mechanisms into three
evolutionary stages: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs),
pure Vision Transformers (ViTs), and emerging Hybrid CNN-
Transformer frameworks. Figure 3 provides a structural taxon-
omy of these approaches, further subdivided by their primary
clinical task and functional mechanisms.
A. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
CNNs remain the foundational backbone of medical image
analysis due to their inherent translation invariance and effi-
ciency in extracting localized spatial hierarchies. Within renal
applications, they are primarily deployed across two functional
domains.
3D Volumetric Segmentation:For spatial boundary map-
ping, the U-Net architecture and its 3D variants have dom-
inated benchmark leaderboards. The symmetric encoder-
decoder structure effectively recovers spatial resolution lost
during downsampling [15]. Recent optimizations focus on
structurally modifying this core. For instance, the ASD-Net
introduces an asymmetric spatial-channel convolution module
to better capture irregular tumor boundaries [16], while other
frameworks integrate Convolutional Block Attention Modules
(CBAM) to suppress background parenchymal noise and high-
light critical regions of interest [17].
Rapid Triage Classification:For multi-class classification,
lightweight CNNs are prioritized for their high-throughput
inference capabilities. Beyond these specialized boundary-
mapping networks, numerous studies have successfully de-
ployed traditional, lightweight CNN variants and ensemble
methods to establish foundational diagnostic baselines for gen-
eral renal abnormality detection [18]–[25]. Architectures like
KidneyNeXt leverage pyramid channel and spatial attention
networks to achieve rapid diagnostic triage on standard clinical
hardware, efficiently categorizing 2D axial slices into normal,
cyst, tumor, or stone classifications [26].
B. Vision Transformers (ViTs)
Despite their success, CNNs are fundamentally limited by
their localized receptive fields. They struggle to model long-
range global dependencies—such as the spatial relationship
between a multi-focal renal cell carcinoma and the broader
urinary tract.
Global Context Modeling:Vision Transformers address
this limitation by treating images as sequences of flattened
patches, applying self-attention mechanisms to weigh the
global context of the entire scan simultaneously [27]. Pure
attention networks allow the model to understand holistic
anatomical relationships.

C. Hybrid CNN-Transformer Architectures
To bridge the gap between local precision and global con-
textual awareness, the current state-of-the-art has converged on
Hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures. These models lever-
age CNN encoders to extract fine-grained, high-resolution
local features and utilize Transformer modules to capture
global semantic relationships.
Cascaded Fusion:Several notable frameworks highlight
this synthesis through sequential processing. The H2Former
introduces a cascaded hybrid network that continuously passes
CNN and Transformer representations back and forth, achiev-
ing superior performance on complex medical image segmen-
tation tasks [28].
Dual Attention & Routing:Other architectures focus on
optimizing the attention bottleneck. The DAE-Former utilizes
a dual attention-guided efficient Transformer to provide multi-
organ contouring with highly accurate spatial mapping [29].
More recently, architectures like BRAU-Net++ have integrated
Bi-Level Routing Attention into a U-shaped topology, sig-
nificantly reducing the quadratic computational complexity
of traditional self-attention while maintaining state-of-the-art
Dice scores on renal benchmarks [30].
By fusing these modalities, hybrid networks represent the
most promising computational foundation for the unified clin-
ical pipelines required in modern nephrology.
V. EXPLAINABILITY ANDCLINICALTRUSTWORTHINESS
State-of-the-art performance on benchmark datasets does
not, by itself, justify clinical deployment. As deep learning
architectures approach diagnostic parity with specialist ra-
diologists in renal imaging tasks, the central question has
shifted fromhow accurate?tohow trustworthy?Explainable
AI (XAI) sits at the heart of this transition: it provides
the interpretive scaffolding through which complex model
decisions can be audited, validated, and ultimately trusted by
the clinicians who act on them. This section examines why
transparency is a non-negotiable requirement in nephrology-
oriented AI, evaluates the practical landscape of post-hoc
visualization and inherently interpretable design, and considers
how these tools together support a collaborative, human-in-the-
loop clinical workflow.

A. Why Explainability Matters in Surgical Planning
High Dice scores and classification accuracies are nec-
essary but insufficient criteria for surgical AI. In nephrol-
ogy, automated outputs directly shape high-stakes procedu-
ral decisions—from preoperative planning for Percutaneous
Nephrolithotomy (PCNL) to intraoperative guidance during
robot-assisted partial nephrectomies [31]. Under these condi-
tions, an unexplained prediction carries genuine clinical risk:
if a model flags a renal mass without indicating which imaging
features drove that classification, the clinician has no reliable
means to confirm or contest it [10].
This accountability gap is not merely philosophical. A
comparative study on stone-free rate prediction demonstrated
that large language models, despite their broad linguistic capa-
bilities, failed to outperform validated clinical scoring systems
precisely because their reasoning chains were opaque and
could not be subjected to clinical scrutiny [32]. The authors
identified non-transparency as a primary barrier to adoption—
a finding that generalises across deep learning modalities in
urological AI. XAI addresses this by transforming black-box
predictions into auditable, spatially grounded justifications,
repositioning automated systems as decision-support tools
rather than autonomous diagnostic agents.
B. Gradient-Based Visualizations: Grad-CAM and Variants
Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)
remains the most widely adopted post-hoc interpretability
technique in renal imaging, owing to its architectural agnos-
ticism and low implementation overhead. By back-projecting
the spatial gradients of the final convolutional layer onto the in-
put image, Grad-CAM generates coarse localization heatmaps
that identify the anatomical regions most influential to a
given prediction. In CT-based renal analysis, this mechanism
offers clinicians a rapid visual sanity check: does the model’s
activation align with the pathological region, or is it responding
to irrelevant background structure?
Multi-center 3D CNN frameworks have leveraged these
maps to establish explainable visual correlations between
lesion size and detection confidence across heterogeneous
imaging protocols [13]. Attention-augmented detectors sim-
ilarly employ spatial and channel attention outputs to confirm
that network focus concentrates on calcified stone deposits
or irregular tumor margins rather than adjacent parenchymal
tissue [33]. The DAE-Former architecture goes a step further,
demonstrating that Transformer-based attention maps can be
inherently gradient-interpretable, providing multi-organ con-
touring outputs with built-in spatial accountability for radiation
dose planning [29].
Nonetheless, gradient-based explanations carry well-
documented limitations. Their spatial resolution is constrained
by the receptive field of the final convolutional stage, and
they are susceptible to highlighting imaging features that are
statistically correlated with the target class without being
causally responsible for the pathology. This distinction—
correlation versus causation in saliency mapping—remains an
open research problem and motivates the exploration of more
structurally integrated interpretability approaches.
C. Inherent Interpretability vs. Post-Hoc Analysis
Post-hoc methods like Grad-CAM and SHAP (SHapley
Additive exPlanations) offer broad applicability across pre-
trained networks, but their explanations are approximations
of decisions already made. A fundamentally different design
philosophy embeds interpretability into the architecture itself,
ensuring that the reasoning process is transparent by construc-
tion rather than reconstructed after the fact.
Convolutional Block Attention Modules (CBAM) and Bi-
Level Routing Attention (BRA), as deployed in hybrid CNN-
Transformer segmentation networks, produce explicit spatial
focus matrices at each feature extraction stage [30]. The prac-
tical implication is that a radiologist reviewing such a model’s
output can trace exactly which spatial regions were weighted
at each processing layer, rather than relying on a single ter-
minal heatmap. This architectural transparency is particularly
valuable in multi-class scenarios: in the CNN-Attention-LSTM
framework for sequential kidney stone detection, the integrated
attention outputs identify high-activation stone positions slice-
by-slice, constructing an auditable diagnostic chain across
volumetric CT series [34].
At the other end of the interpretability spectrum, classi-
cal feature engineering approaches—such as Gray-Level Co-
occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture analysis combined with
statistical feature selection (POE+ACC)—offer fully determin-
istic, rule-based transparency [14]. This drive for architec-
tural transparency, alternative structured feature extraction, and
domain-adapted text-vision models is similarly documented
across a wide swath of earlier computational frameworks
and hybrid benchmarks [35]–[42]. These methods produce
inherently interpretable decision boundaries but sacrifice the
semantic depth and contextual sensitivity that deep represen-
tations provide. In practice, neither extreme is universally op-
timal; the appropriate balance depends on the clinical task, the
regulatory environment, and the degree of radiologist oversight
available. Hybrid approaches that pair deep visual features
with structured, interpretable output heads represent the most
pragmatic direction for near-term clinical deployment.
D. Human-AI Collaboration and Standardisation
The practical goal of XAI in renal diagnostics is not to
make every model decision comprehensible in isolation, but
to calibrate how much autonomy clinicians should delegate
to automated systems across different task types. For routine
multi-class triage on standard CT protocols, a well-validated
model with Grad-CAM outputs may provide sufficient trans-
parency. For edge cases—atypical tumor morphologies, multi-
component calculi, or post-operative anatomy—clinician over-
ride supported by detailed attention maps becomes essential.
Embedding XAI into the clinical workflow shifts the ra-
diologist’s role from exhaustive manual screening to targeted
validation of AI-highlighted anomalies [15]. This redistribu-
tion of cognitive load is clinically meaningful: it reduces
interpretation fatigue in high-volume imaging centres while
preserving the specialist judgment that automated systems
cannot yet replicate. The limiting factor is not technical capa-
bility but the absence of standardised interpretability protocols.

VI. A PROPOSEDUNIFIEDCLINICALFRAMEWORK
The preceding sections have documented substantial
progress across individual diagnostic tasks—classification,
segmentation, NLP, and interpretability—yet the practical gap
between algorithmic benchmarks and operational clinical tools
remains wide. This section pivots from retrospective synthesis
to a prospective architectural proposal. Serving as a roadmap
for future researchers, this proposed pipeline is a conceptual
synthesis of the best-performing models identified in Sec-
tion IV. We introduce a theoretical three-stage multimodal
framework designed to unify renal triage, surgical boundary
mapping, and automated clinical reporting within a single
coherent pipeline, culminating in an interactive dashboard that
addresses both the computational and workflow demands of
active nephrological practice.
A. The Three-Stage Clinical Gap
The fragmentation of current renal AI is not merely a
modelling limitation—it reflects a systemic disconnect in how
the field has framed the clinical problem. Classification net-
works are optimised for category discrimination; segmentation
models for spatial precision; NLP systems for unstructured
text. None of these, individually, addresses the full diagnostic
cycle that a nephrologist must navigate:What is the pathology?
Where exactly are its margins? And what does the patient’s
clinical history tell us about management?
A model that achieves 99% accuracy on the Kaggle CT
Kidney dataset [44] provides no direct guidance on whether a
2.8 cm renal mass requires immediate surgical intervention
or active surveillance. Conversely, a highly accurate vol-
umetric segmentation model for the KiTS challenges [11]
produces surgical masks without the accompanying multi-
class discrimination needed to guide pre-operative planning
for heterogeneous pathologies. These isolated strengths, when
combined with the lack of native integration with patient
health records, produce a workflow that demands substan-
tial radiologist synthesis—precisely the burden that clinical
AI should reduce. The conceptual framework proposed here
(Figure 4) explicitly targets this translational gap by treating
detection, surgical mapping, and automated reporting as de-
pendent, sequentially coupled stages rather than independent
computational problems.
B. Stage 1: Automated Renal Pathology Classification
The first stage functions as a high-throughput triage
gate. Hospital Picture Archiving and Communication Sys-
tems (PACS) process substantial daily imaging volumes, and
the practical viability of any AI-assisted workflow depends
on architectures lightweight enough to operate within stan-
dard clinical computational budgets. Drawing on the Kid-
neyNeXt backbone augmented with Pyramid Channel and
Spatial Attention (PCSA) modules [26], [45], this stage
would theoretically perform rapid multi-class categorisation
based on the benchmark capabilities of these architectures
across the four clinically relevant renal pathology categories—
normal parenchyma, complex cysts, calcified stones, and solid
malignancies—on standard 12,446-image CT datasets.
Critically, this classification stage is not an end-point but a
gating mechanism. Normal findings are logged and discharged
from further processing; critical findings—tumour or obstruct-
ing calculus detections—conditionally route the imaging data
to Stage 2. This conditional architecture ensures that com-
putationally expensive segmentation resources are deployed
selectively, preserving system throughput without sacrificing
diagnostic coverage.
C. Stage 2: Real-Time Surgical Boundary Segmentation
Preoperative planning for robot-assisted partial nephrectomy
or PCNL demands spatial precision that extends well be-
yond what classification outputs provide. Surgeons require
exact tumour margins, stone dimensions, and proximity to
collecting system structures to minimise parenchymal sacrifice
and reduce vascular complication risk. This stage addresses
that need through real-time instance segmentation, propos-
ing YOLOv11-based architectures adapted for medical imag-
ing [46].
The case for a YOLO-family approach over traditional
3D U-Nets rests on practical clinical deployment constraints.
While architectures such as ASD-Net [16] or the spatial-
channel attention enhanced U-Net [17] deliver strong Dice
performance on KiTS benchmarks, their 3D volumetric con-
volutions introduce inference latencies that are incompatible
with real-time surgical guidance. A streamlined single-stage
detector, operating on axial CT slices with CBAM-augmented
attention, translates bounding box predictions into actionable
surgical boundary masks at frame rates appropriate for intra-
operative use, delivering the low-latency spatial feedback that
the clinical environment demands.
D. Stage 3: Clinical NLP Integration
The third stage addresses a dimension of clinical AI that
the imaging literature has largely treated as peripheral: the
structured representation of unstructured clinical knowledge.
Radiological reports, referring physician notes, and laboratory
results contain contextual information—prior stone episodes,
family history of renal cell carcinoma, eGFR trajectories—that
no amount of CT pixel analysis can replicate. Integrating this
textual context into the diagnostic workflow requires domain-
adapted biomedical language models.
By deploying PubMedBERT [47] fine-tuned for clinical
Named Entity Recognition (NER), this stage would theoreti-
cally ingest the unstructured patient health record alongside the
visual metadata produced in Stages 1 and 2. The NLP engine

E. The Integrated Clinical Dashboard
The technical outputs of the three stages described above
are clinically meaningful only if they are accessible and
interpretable at the point of care. The proposed dashboard
consolidates four information streams into a single interface:
the original DICOM CT slice; the Grad-CAM interpretability
overlay from Stage 1; the YOLO-generated surgical boundary
masks from Stage 2; and the structured clinical metadata
compiled by Stage 3. This co-presentation is deliberate—
it enables the reviewing nephrologist or urologist to cross-
validate the automated findings against the original imaging
and the patient’s clinical context without navigating multiple
systems.
The design principle underlying this dashboard is epistemic
transparency: every automated output is accompanied by its
visual justification, and the clinician retains final authority at
each decision point. This architecture directly addresses the
accountability concerns raised in Section V, embedding XAI
not as a post-development afterthought but as a structural
component of the clinical interface. Integration with HL7
FHIR standards ensures that dashboard outputs feed directly
into existing hospital information systems, avoiding the man-
ual transcription overhead that represents one of the most
persistent barriers to AI adoption in clinical settings [13].
VII. REAL-WORLDDEPLOYMENTCHALLENGES
Demonstrating strong performance on curated benchmark
datasets is a necessary but insufficient condition for clinical
deployment. The gap between a model that achieves 99%
accuracy on the Kaggle CT Kidney dataset and one that func-
tions reliably across the heterogeneous conditions of a busy
district general hospital is substantial, and the literature has
only recently begun to address it systematically. This section
examines the three most consequential barriers: distributional
generalisation, the mismatch between algorithmic and clinical
metrics, and the engineering complexity of PACS integration.
A. Data Generalisation and Modality Shifts
The majority of high-performing renal AI models are trained
and validated on a small number of well-curated repositories.
The Kaggle CT Kidney dataset [44], [49] and the KiTS
challenge cohorts [11] have been invaluable for establishing
algorithmic baselines, but their controlled acquisition condi-
tions do not reflect the scanner heterogeneity encountered in
routine clinical imaging. Slice thickness, reconstruction kernel,
contrast phase, and vendor-specific HU calibration all vary

8
substantially across institutions, and these variations system-
atically degrade model performance in ways that benchmark
evaluations do not capture [15].
This reliance on homogeneous training data or single-
modality captures is a prevalent limitation observed across
a broad spectrum of current multi-task, super-resolution, and
volumetric segmentation literature [50]–[57].
The multi-phase nature of renal CT protocols compounds
this problem. Models trained primarily on portal venous
or nephrographic phase acquisitions frequently fail on non-
contrast studies, where calculi are most visible but soft-tissue
contrast is limited, and on arterial phase acquisitions, where
vascular anatomy introduces confounding HU patterns [13],
[58]. Without prospective multi-centre validation incorporating
the full range of acquisition protocols encountered in clinical
practice, published performance figures should be interpreted
with caution. The models reviewed in this survey that ex-
plicitly report external validation—notably the multi-centre
3D CNN of Liang et al. [13] and the DBAR-Net of Sharon
and Anbarasi [59]—represent the direction that the field must
move, but they remain the exception rather than the rule.
B. Algorithmic vs. Clinical Metrics
The standard performance vocabulary of deep learning—
Dice Similarity Coefficient, Intersection over Union, macro
F1-score—was designed to measure spatial or categorical
alignment between model outputs and annotated ground truth.
These metrics are well-suited to comparing architectures on a
fixed dataset, but they are poor proxies for the outcomes that
matter clinically.
Consider a model that achieves a Dice score of 0.92 on kid-
ney tumour segmentation. That figure may reflect near-perfect
delineation of a large, well-defined clear cell carcinoma while
entirely missing a 4 mm satellite lesion at the tumour margin—
a miss that has negligible impact on the aggregate score but
significant implications for resection completeness [17], [60].
Equally, a stone detection model reporting 96% accuracy may
perform adequately on stones larger than 5 mm while failing
on sub-3 mm calculi that are clinically relevant for PCNL
access planning [58]. The disconnect is systematic: optimising
for pixel-level metrics during training does not guarantee that
the resulting model behaviour aligns with surgical priorities.
Addressing this requires a fundamental shift in how renal
AI is evaluated. Clinical validation studies should incorpo-
rate task-specific metrics: stone-free rate prediction, surgical
margin clearance rates, reduction in time-to-report, and inter-
observer variability across radiologist cohorts [61]. Progress in
this direction is slow—partly because such endpoints require
prospective clinical trial infrastructure—but it is a prerequisite
for regulatory approval and sustainable clinical adoption.
C. PACS Integration and Computational Overhead
Even a model that satisfies both generalisation and clin-
ical validity requirements faces a third class of challenge:
the practical engineering of integration into legacy hospital
infrastructure. Most deployed clinical imaging systems were
not designed with deep learning inference in mind, and
retrofitting AI processing into existing PACS workflows intro-
duces non-trivial latency, data governance, and interoperability
constraints.
Large-scale 3D architectures—volumetric U-Net variants,
multi-scale Vision Transformers—typically require enterprise-
grade GPU hardware (e.g., NVIDIA A100 or Tesla-class
accelerators) to maintain inference speeds compatible with
clinical workflows [29], [31]. The majority of district-level
clinical environments lack this infrastructure, and even where
it exists, the overhead of routing DICOM data to GPU-
equipped inference servers introduces latency that may be
unacceptable for time-sensitive applications such as emer-
gency renal colic triage [58]. Model compression techniques—
quantisation, knowledge distillation, and pruning—offer par-
tial mitigation, but their application to medical imaging models
requires careful validation to ensure that compression does
not disproportionately degrade performance on the rare but
clinically critical edge cases [10].
Interoperability presents a parallel challenge. Clinical AI
outputs must be delivered in formats that integrate natively
with hospital information systems and downstream docu-
mentation workflows. The HL7 FHIR standard provides a
structured mechanism for this integration, and its adoption by
frameworks such as the multi-centre urinary disease detection
system of Liang et al. [13] demonstrates that PACS-compatible
deployment is achievable. Scaling this approach across diverse
institutional environments, each with different legacy system
configurations, nonetheless remains a substantial engineering
undertaking that the research community has yet to address
systematically [62].
VIII. CONCLUSION
A. Summary of Findings
This survey has traced the progression of deep learning in
renal imaging from early CNN-based multi-class classifiers
through to the current generation of hybrid Vision Transformer
and attention-augmented architectures. Several clear trajecto-
ries emerge from the literature. First, raw classification and
segmentation accuracy—while still improving—is no longer
the primary differentiator between architectures; the field is
increasingly stratified by computational efficiency, architec-
tural interpretability, and demonstrated robustness to domain
shift. Second, the adoption of Explainable AI has moved from
optional enhancement to practical necessity: models deployed
in surgical planning contexts without transparent decision
mechanisms face significant barriers to clinical acceptance,
regardless of their benchmark performance. Third, and most
significantly, the prevailing model-centric framing of renal
AI—in which each architecture is evaluated in isolation on a
static dataset—is increasingly misaligned with the integrated,
multimodal nature of actual clinical workflows.
The reviewed literature also reveals persistent structural
gaps. Multi-centre external validation remains rare. Clinical
outcome metrics are seldom reported alongside algorithmic
ones. NLP integration with imaging pipelines is largely theo-
retical in existing publications. These gaps are not incidental;
they reflect the boundary between what is tractable as a
--- PAGE 8 ---
9
machine learning problem and what is required for clinical
translation.
B. Clinical Outlook
The trajectory of renal AI points unambiguously toward
multimodal integration. The isolated successes documented
across classification, segmentation, and language modelling
are necessary precursors, but they become clinically trans-
formative only when composed into coherent diagnostic
pipelines. The unified framework proposed in Section VI
represents one such composition: a sequentially coupled sys-
tem in which lightweight classification gates downstream
segmentation, segmentation outputs inform report generation,
and all findings are presented through an interpretable, PACS-
integrated interface.
Realising this vision requires progress on multiple fronts
simultaneously—generalisation, evaluation methodology, and
systems engineering—none of which can be solved by al-
gorithmic innovation alone. It demands prospective clinical
collaborations, standardised benchmarking protocols, and reg-
ulatory frameworks adapted to the iterative nature of deployed
AI. The role of the nephrologist and urologist in this process is
not diminished by automation; it is recentred. Human clinical
judgment remains the validating authority for AI outputs, and
designing systems that honour that authority—through trans-
parency, appropriate uncertainty communication, and genuine
human override capacity—is the most consequential design
challenge facing renal AI today.
